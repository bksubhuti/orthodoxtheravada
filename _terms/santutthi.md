### Grammatical Analysis
**Santuṭṭhi**: [f.] contentment; satisfaction. Derived from prefix *saṃ* (completely) + root *tus* (to be satisfied, pleased). The Aṅguttara-ṭīkā explains it: *sammā vā ñāyena bhagavatā anuññātavidhinā paccayehi tuṭṭhi santuṭṭhi*, "contentment is satisfaction with the requisites rightly, by the method permitted by the Blessed One; in meaning, it is contentment with any kind of requisite (*itarītarapaccayasantoso*)" (Mp-t-ix 327).

### Orthodox Definition
In the Mūla, the Ariyavaṃsa Sutta (AN 4.28) says that a bhikkhu "is content with any kind of robe (*itarītarena cīvarena*), and speaks in praise of contentment with any kind of robe; he does not engage in a wrong search because of robes; if he does not get a robe he is not agitated, and if he gets one he uses it without being tied to it, seeing the danger and understanding the escape; he does not extol himself or disparage others because of that contentment". The same is said of alms food and lodging, and the fourth lineage is delight in development and in abandoning (A-iv 309–311).

The Manorathapūraṇī explains the term: "*santuṭṭhi* means the contentment, called non-greed (*alobha*), that arises in one who attends the content" (Mp-i 306), and the Suttanipāta-aṭṭhakathā: "*santuṭṭhi* is contentment with any kind of requisite (*itarītarapaccayasantoso*); it is twelvefold": for each of robes, alms food, lodging and medicine there are three kinds, *yathālābhasantoso*, *yathābalasantoso* and *yathāsāruppasantoso* (Pj-ii 1329). The Manorathapūraṇī defines them: for one who can subsist on anything, coarse or refined, subsisting on whatever is obtained is *yathālābha*; knowing one's own strength, subsisting on what one is able to subsist on is *yathābala*; giving the pleasing thing to another and subsisting on whatever one has oneself is *yathāsāruppa* (Mp-iv 1134). In its longer explanation (Mp-i 307) the one who is weak or sick and wears a heavy robe exchanges it with a like-minded bhikkhu for a light one, and the one who receives costly robes gives them to the elders, the learned, the sick and those with few gains and makes his own robe of their old rags, and both remain content.

The Dhammapada says: "health is the highest gain, contentment is the greatest wealth" (*ārogyaparamā lābhā, santuṭṭhiparamaṃ dhanaṃ*, Dhp 204).

### Quote (Mūla: Aṅguttara Nikāya)
> **“Cattārome, bhikkhave, ariyavaṃsā aggaññā rattaññā vaṃsaññā porāṇā asaṃkiṇṇā asaṃkiṇṇapubbā, na saṃkīyanti na saṃkīyissanti, appaṭikuṭṭhā samaṇehi brāhmaṇehi viññūhi. Katame cattāro? Idha, bhikkhave, bhikkhu santuṭṭho hoti itarītarena cīvarena, itarītaracīvarasantuṭṭhiyā ca vaṇṇavādī, na ca cīvarahetu anesanaṃ appatirūpaṃ āpajjati, aladdhā ca cīvaraṃ na paritassati, laddhā ca cīvaraṃ agadhito amucchito anajjhosanno ādīnavadassāvī nissaraṇapañño paribhuñjati; tāya ca pana itarītaracīvarasantuṭṭhiyā nevattānukkaṃseti, no paraṃ vambheti.**  
> 28. “Monks, there are these four noble lineages, primal, of long standing, traditional, ancient, unadulterated and never before adulterated, which are not being adulterated and will not be adulterated, which are not repudiated by wise ascetics and brahmins. What four? Here, monks, a bhikkhu is content with any kind of robe, and he speaks in praise of contentment with any kind of robe, and he does not engage in a wrong search, in what is improper, because of robes. If he does not get a robe he is not agitated, and if he gets one he uses it without being tied to it, infatuated with it, or blindly absorbed in it, seeing the danger and understanding the escape. Yet he does not extol himself or disparage others because of that contentment with any kind of robe.  
>

### Quote (Aṭṭhakathā: Suttanipāta-aṭṭhakathā)
> **Santuṭṭhi nāma itarītarapaccayasantoso, so dvādasavidho hoti. Seyyathidaṃ – cīvare yathālābhasantoso, yathābalasantoso, yathāsāruppasantosoti tividho. Evaṃ piṇḍapātādīsu.**  
> 'Santuṭṭhī' (contentment) means being satisfied with whatever is obtained; it is of twelve types. That is to say—contentment with robes is of three types: contentment with whatever is obtained, contentment according to one's strength, and contentment that is appropriate. Likewise for almsfood and so on.  
>  
> — *Paramatthajotikā* (Suttanipāta-aṭṭhakathā, Maṅgala Sutta) · [TPR](https://tpr.pali.tools/open?book_id=attha_ku_05_02&page_number=27&name=suttanip%C4%81taa%E1%B9%AD%E1%B9%ADhakath%C4%81+%28du%29&note=&selected_text=U2FudHXhua3hua1oaSBuxIFtYSBpdGFyxKt0YXJhcGFjY2F5YXNhbnRvc28sIHNvIGR2xIFkYXNhdmlkaG8gaG90aS4%3D)

### Quote (Aṭṭhakathā: Manorathapūraṇī)
> **Lūkhapaṇītesu yena kenaci yāpetuṃ sakkontassa yathāladdheneva yāpanaṃ yathālābhasantoso nāma. Attano thāmaṃ jānitvā yena yāpetuṃ sakkoti, tena yāpanaṃ yathābalasantoso nāma. Manāpaṃ aññassa datvā attanā yena kenaci yāpanaṃ yathāsāruppasantoso nāma.**  
> For one able to subsist on anything among coarse or refined things, subsisting on whatever is obtained is called ‘contentment by whatever is obtained.’ Knowing one's own strength and subsisting on whatever one is able to subsist on is called ‘contentment by strength.’ Giving a pleasant thing to another and subsisting on whatever one has oneself is called ‘contentment by appropriateness.’  
>  
> — *Manorathapūraṇī* (Aṅguttara-aṭṭhakathā, Ariyavaṃsa Sutta) · [TPR](https://tpr.pali.tools/open?book_id=attha_an_02&page_number=279&name=duk%C4%81dinip%C4%81taa%E1%B9%AD%E1%B9%ADhakath%C4%81&note=&selected_text=TMWra2hhcGHhuYfEq3Rlc3UgeWVuYSBrZW5hY2kgecSBcGV0deG5gyBzYWtrb250YXNzYSB5YXRoxIFsYWRkaGVuZXZh)
