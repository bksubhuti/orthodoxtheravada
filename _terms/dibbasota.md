### Grammatical Analysis
**Dibbasota**: [nt.] the divine ear; the divine ear element (*dibbasotadhātu*). Formed by *dibba* (divine) + *sota* (ear). The Visuddhimagga explains *dibba*: it is divine because it is like the divine ear element that deities have as a result of good conduct, capable of receiving an object even from afar; because it is obtained by way of a divine abiding; and it is *sotadhātu* in the sense of hearing and of being soulless (*savanaṭṭhena nijjīvaṭṭhena ca sotadhātu*, Vism-ii 153:4-9).

### Orthodox Definition
*Dibbasota* is the knowledge of the divine ear element. In the Mūla, a bhikkhu whose mind is concentrated, pure, bright, free from defects and imperturbable directs it to the divine ear element, and "with the divine ear element, which is purified and surpasses the human, he hears both kinds of sounds, the divine and the human, those which are far and those which are near" (D-i 349:1-2, in the Sāmaññaphala Sutta). The same words are used of the Buddha in the Mahāsīhanāda Sutta (M-i 447:2) and appear in the Ākaṅkheyya Sutta (M-i 180:2).

The Aṭṭhakathā explains the terms: *visuddhā* is "quite pure and without imperfections"; *atikkantamānusikā* means that it has surpassed the human fleshly ear element; "both kinds of sounds" are those of devas and humans, and "far and near" means sounds even in another world-system and, among those near, even the sounds of creatures within his own body (Vism-ii 154:1-8).

As to the way of producing it, the Visuddhimagga says that the bhikkhu enters the jhāna that is the basis for the higher knowledges (*abhiññāpādakajjhāna*), emerges, and with the preliminary-work concentration adverts first to a gross sound far away within normal hearing, then successively to subtler sounds (the sounds in the monastery, the voices of those reciting, birds, wind, footsteps and so on), in each direction, until the sounds become exceedingly clear (Vism-ii 155:1-8). Then mind-door adverting arises, followed by four or five impulsions, the last being the fine-material absorption consciousness of the fourth jhāna; "the knowledge that has arisen together with that absorption consciousness should be known as the divine ear element" (Vism-ii 156:1-3).

### Quote (Mūla: Dīgha Nikāya)
> **“So evaṃ samāhite citte parisuddhe pariyodāte anaṅgaṇe vigatūpakkilese mudubhūte kammaniye ṭhite āneñjappatte dibbāya sotadhātuyā cittaṃ abhinīharati abhininnāmeti. So dibbāya sotadhātuyā visuddhāya atikkantamānusikāya ubho sadde suṇāti dibbe ca mānuse ca ye dūre santike ca.**  
> 240. When his mind is thus concentrated, pure and bright, unblemished, free from defects, malleable, wieldy, steady, and attained to imperturbability, he directs and inclines it to the divine ear-element. With the divine ear-element, which is purified and surpasses the human, he hears both kinds of sound, the divine and the human, those which are distant and those which are near.  
>

### Quote (Mūla: Majjhima Nikāya)
> **“Ayampi hi nāma, sāriputta, sunakkhattassa moghapurisassa mayi dhammanvayo na bhavissati – ‘ itipi so bhagavā dibbāya sotadhātuyā visuddhāya atikkantamānusikāya ubho sadde suṇāti – dibbe ca mānuse ca, ye dūre santike cā ’ ti.**  
> “Sāriputta, this misguided man Sunakkhatta will never infer of me according to Dhamma: ‘That Blessed One hears both kinds of sounds, the heavenly and the human, those that are far as well as near, with the divine ear element, which is purified and surpasses human (hearing).’  
>

### Quote (Aṭṭhakathā: Visuddhimagga (the meaning of the term))
> **Tatra dibbāya sotadhātuyāti ettha dibbasadisattā dibbā. Devānaṃ hi sucaritakammanibbattā pittasemharuhirādīhi apalibuddhā upakkilesavimuttatāya dūrepi ārammaṇaṃ sampaṭicchanasamatthā dibbapasādasotadhātu hoti. Ayañcāpi imassa bhikkhuno vīriyabhāvanābalanibbattā ñāṇasotadhātu tādisāyevāti dibbasadisattā dibbā. Apica dibbavihāravasena paṭiladdhattā attanā ca dibbavihārasannissitattāpi dibbā. Savanaṭṭhena nijjīvaṭṭhena ca sotadhātu. Sotadhātukiccakaraṇena ca sotadhātu viyātipi sotadhātu. Tāya dibbāya sotadhātuyā. Visuddhāyāti parisuddhāya nirupakkilesāya. Atikkantamānusikāyāti manussūpacāraṃ atikkamitvā saddasavanena mānusikaṃ maṃsasotadhātuṃ atikkantāya vītivattitvā ṭhitāya. Ubho sadde suṇātīti dve sadde suṇāti. Katame dve? Dibbe ca mānuse ca, devānañca manussānañca saddeti vuttaṃ hoti. Etena padesapariyādānaṃ veditabbaṃ. Ye dūre santike cāti ye saddā dūre paracakkavāḷepi ye ca santike antamaso sadehasannissitapāṇakasaddāpi, te suṇātīti vuttaṃ hoti. Etena nippadesapariyādānaṃ veditabbaṃ.**  
> Therein, in the phrase with the divine ear element: it is “divine” (dibbā) due to being divine-like. For deities have a divine sensitive ear-element that is produced by kamma of good conduct, is unimpeded by bile, phlegm, blood, and so on, and due to being liberated from imperfections, is capable of receiving an object even from afar. And this ear-element of knowledge, produced by the power of this bhikkhu's development of energy, is just like that; thus, it is “divine” because it is divine-like. Furthermore, it is divine because it is obtained by way of the divine abiding and because he himself has the divine abiding as his support. It is an ear-element in the sense of hearing and in the sense of being soulless. And it is an ear-element because it is like an ear-element by performing the function of an ear-element. With that divine ear element. Which is purified (visuddhāyā) means quite pure and without imperfections. Surpassing human (atikkantamānusikāyā) means that by the hearing of sounds, having surpassed the human proximity, it has surpassed the human fleshly ear-element and stands beyond it. He hears both kinds of sounds means he hears two kinds of sounds. What two? Divine and human: this means the sounds of devas and of humans. By this, partial inclusion should be understood. Those that are far as well as near: this means he hears those sounds that are far away, even in another cosmos, and those that are near, even down to the sounds of the creatures dwelling in his own body. By this, complete inclusion should be understood.  
>  
> — [Visuddhimagga](/glossary/visuddhimagga/) (Abhiññāniddesa, Dibbasotadhātukathā, Vism-ii 152-154) · [TPR](https://tpr.pali.tools/open?book_id=annya_bi_02&page_number=37&name=visuddhimaggo+%28du%29&note=&selected_text=VGF0cmEgZGliYsSBeWEgc290YWRoxIF0dXnEgXRpIGV0dGhhIGRpYmJhc2FkaXNhdHTEgSBkaWJixIEu)


### Quote (Aṭṭhakathā: Visuddhimagga (how it is produced))
> **Kathaṃ panāyaṃ uppādetabbāti? Tena bhikkhunā abhiññāpādakajjhānaṃ samāpajjitvā vuṭṭhāya parikammasamādhicittena paṭhamataraṃ pakatisotapathe dūre oḷāriko araññe sīhādīnaṃ saddo āvajjitabbo. Vihāre ghaṇḍisaddo, bherisaddo, saṅkhasaddo, sāmaṇeradaharabhikkhūnaṃ sabbathāmena sajjhāyantānaṃ sajjhāyanasaddo, pakatikathaṃ kathentānaṃ “kiṃ bhante, kimāvuso” tiādisaddo, sakuṇasaddo, vātasaddo, padasaddo, pakkuthitaudakassa cicciṭāyanasaddo, ātape sussamānatālapaṇṇasaddo, kunthakipillikādisaddoti evaṃ sabboḷārikato pabhuti yathākkamena sukhumasaddā āvajjitabbā.**  
> But how is this to be produced? That bhikkhu, having entered into the jhāna that is the basis for direct-knowledge and having risen, should first advert with the mind of preliminary-work concentration to a gross sound far away in the forest, such as that of lions and so on, within the normal range of hearing. In the monastery: the sound of a gong, the sound of a drum, the sound of a conch, the sound of recitation by novices and young bhikkhus reciting with all their strength, the sound of ordinary talk such as “What, venerable sir?” or “What, friend?”, the sound of birds, the sound of wind, the sound of footsteps, the fizzing sound of boiling water, the sound of palm leaves drying in the sun, the sound of ants, and so on; in this way, beginning with the grossest, he should advert to subtle sounds in succession.  
>  
> — [Visuddhimagga](/glossary/visuddhimagga/) (Abhiññāniddesa, Dibbasotadhātukathā, Vism-ii 155) · [TPR](https://tpr.pali.tools/open?book_id=annya_bi_02&page_number=37&name=visuddhimaggo+%28du%29&note=&selected_text=S2F0aGHhuYMgcGFuxIF5YeG5gyB1cHDEgWRldGFiYsSBdGk%2F)
