### Grammatical Analysis
**Hetupaccaya**: [m.] root condition. Formed by *hetu* (root, cause) + *paccaya* (condition). The Paṭṭhāna Aṭṭhakathā: "that which is a cause (*hetu*) and also a condition (*paccayo*) is *hetupaccayo*; it means 'a condition by way of being a root'" (*hetu ca so paccayo cāti hetupaccayo; hetu hutvā paccayo, hetubhāvena paccayo*, Paṭṭh-a 1917:1-2). *Hetu* here is not "word-part" (as in the world) nor "reason" (as in "ye dhammā hetuppabhavā"), but "root" (*mūlaṃ*), as in "three wholesome roots, three unwholesome roots" (Paṭṭh-a 1917:4-8). *Paccaya* is "that from which, dependent on it, something comes forth", a phenomenon that persists or arises without disregarding another (Paṭṭh-a 1917:9-10); by characteristic it is "the helpful" (*upakārakalakkhaṇo*, Paṭṭh-a 1918:1).

### Orthodox Definition
*Hetupaccaya* is the first of the twenty-four conditions (*paccayā*) enumerated in the Paṭṭhāna's Paccayuddesa (Paṭṭh-i 8:1) and in the Abhidhammatthasaṅgaha's *paṭṭhānanayo* (Abhidh-s 709:1). The Mūla defines it: "Root condition: the roots are related to the states associated with the roots, and to the materiality produced by them, by root condition" (*hetū hetusampayuttakānaṃ dhammānaṃ taṃsamuṭṭhānānañca rūpānaṃ hetupaccayena paccayo*, Paṭṭh-i 10:1).

The Aṭṭhakathā summarises: "in the sense of a root it is a cause, in the sense of being helpful it is a condition; in brief, the phenomenon that helps in the sense of a root is the root condition" (Paṭṭh-a 1918:4). On what the rooting does, it adds: phenomena that have received a root condition are "firm and well established, like trees with flourishing roots, whereas rootless ones are not well established, like moss resembling sesame seeds" (*Laddhahetupaccayā hi dhammā viruḷhamūlā viya pādapā thirā honti suppatiṭṭhitā, ahetukā pana tilabījakādisevālā viya na suppatiṭṭhitā*, Paṭṭh-a 1919:5; the same at Vism-ii 919:2).

The roots are named in the Abhidhammatthasaṅgaha: greed, hatred and delusion are the three unwholesome roots, and non-greed, non-hatred and non-delusion are likewise the wholesome and the indeterminate (Abhidh-s 213:1-216:1). The Dhammasaṅgaṇī has "three wholesome roots, three unwholesome roots, three indeterminate roots" (Dhs 1830:2).

### Quote (Aṭṭhakathā: Paṭṭhāna-aṭṭhakathā)
> **Tattha hetu ca so paccayo cāti hetupaccayo. Hetu hutvā paccayo, hetubhāvena paccayoti vuttaṃ hoti. “Tayo kusalā hetū, tayo akusalā hetū” tiādīsu (dha. sa.1059) mūlaṃ hetūti vuccati. Taṃ idha adhippetaṃ. Iti mūlaṭṭhena hetu, upakārakaṭṭhena paccayoti saṅkhepato mūlaṭṭhena upakārako dhammo hetupaccayo. Laddhahetupaccayā hi dhammā viruḷhamūlā viya pādapā thirā honti suppatiṭṭhitā, ahetukā pana tilabījakādisevālā viya na suppatiṭṭhitā. Iti mūlaṭṭhena upakārakoti suppatiṭṭhitabhāvasādhanena upakārako dhammo hetupaccayoti veditabbo.**  
> There, that which is a cause and also a condition is the cause condition. It means 'having been a cause, it is a condition, or it is a condition by way of being a cause'. In expressions like 'there are three wholesome roots, there are three unwholesome roots' and so on, 'root' is called 'cause'. That is what is intended here. Thus, in the sense of a root, it is a cause; in the sense of being helpful, it is a condition. So, in summary, the phenomenon that is helpful in the sense of a root is the cause condition. Indeed, phenomena that have attained cause conditions are firm and well-established like trees with flourishing roots, but rootless (phenomena) are not well-established like moss resembling sesame seeds and so on. Thus, the phenomenon that is helpful in the sense of a root is the cause condition, which should be understood by establishing the state of being well-established as helpful.  
>  
> — Paṭṭhāna-aṭṭhakathā (Paṭṭh-a 1917-1919, Paccayuddesavaṇṇanā) · [TPR](https://tpr.pali.tools/open?book_id=attha_bi_03&page_number=344&name=pa%C3%B1capakara%E1%B9%87aa%E1%B9%AD%E1%B9%ADhakath%C4%81&note=&selected_text=VGF0dGhhIGhldHUgY2Egc28gcGFjY2F5byBjxIF0aQ%3D%3D)
