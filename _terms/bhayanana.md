### Grammatical Analysis
**Bhayañāṇa**: [nt.] knowledge of fear, a compound of *bhaya* (fear, terror) + *ñāṇa* (knowledge). The Mūla names it *bhayatupaṭṭhāne paññā ādīnave ñāṇaṃ*, "the wisdom in the appearance as terror is knowledge of danger" (Paṭis 13:1, 310:1); the Visuddhimagga heads the section "*Bhayatupaṭṭhānañāṇakathā*" (Vism-ii 1709:1). Abhidhammatthasaṅgaha lists it as *bhayañāṇaṃ* among the ten insight knowledges (Abhidh-s 815:1).

### Orthodox Definition
In the Paṭisambhidāmagga, the knowledge is described as seeing each thing as terror: "arising is terror, occurrence is terror, the sign is terror, accumulation is terror, rebirth-linking is terror ... birth, old age, sickness, death, grief, lamentation, despair are terror: this wisdom in the appearance as terror is knowledge of danger"; the opposite, "non-arising is safety", is knowledge of the peaceful state (Paṭis 310:1-4, 311:1-2).

The Visuddhimagga says that when the meditator develops contemplation of dissolution, formations in all becomings, wombs, destinations, stations and abodes appear to him as fearful, like a lion or a tiger to a timid man; and, seeing "past formations have ceased, present ones are ceasing, and those that will arise in the future will cease likewise", the knowledge of the appearance as fear arises (Vism-ii 1710:1-2). The Aṭṭhakathā gives similes: the woman whose three sons were condemned (Vism-ii 1711:1-6), and the woman who bore ten children, nine dead, one dying in her hand, one in the womb, who gave up attachment to the one in the womb (Vism-ii 1712:1-5).

The Visuddhimagga asks whether this knowledge itself is afraid, and answers: it is not; it is only a judgment (*tīraṇamattameva*) that past formations have ceased, present ones are ceasing and future ones will cease, like a man with good sight looking at three pits of burning coals at the city gate, who is not afraid himself (Vism-ii 1713:1-4). The Paṭisambhidāmagga-aṭṭhakathā adds that for one who cultivates this knowledge no protection, no refuge, no resort appears in the three becomings, the four modes of birth, the five destinations, the seven stations of consciousness or the nine abodes of beings, and the three becomings appear as pits of burning coals (Paṭis-a 124:2).

### Quote (Mūla: Paṭisambhidāmagga)
> **Kathaṃ bhayatupaṭṭhāne paññā ādīnave ñāṇaṃ? Uppādo bhayanti – bhayatupaṭṭhāne paññā ādīnave ñāṇaṃ. Pavattaṃ bhayanti – bhayatupaṭṭhāne paññā ādīnave ñāṇaṃ.**  
> 53. How is it that wisdom in the appearance as terror is knowledge of danger? ‘Arising is terror’—this wisdom in the appearance as terror is knowledge of danger. ‘Occurrence is terror’—this wisdom in the appearance as terror is knowledge of danger.

### Quote (Aṭṭhakathā: Visuddhimagga)
> **Tassevaṃ sabbasaṅkhārānaṃ khayavayabhedanirodhārammaṇaṃ bhaṅgānupassanaṃ āsevantassa bhāventassa bahulīkarontassa sabbabhavayonigatiṭhitisattāvāsesu pabhedakā saṅkhārā sukhena jīvitukāmassa bhīrukapurisassa sīhabyagghadīpiacchataracchayakkharakkhasacaṇḍagoṇacaṇḍakukkurapabhinnamadacaṇḍahatthighoraāsīvisaasanivicakkasusānaraṇabhūmijalitaaṅgārakāsuādayo viya mahābhayaṃ hutvā upaṭṭhahanti. Tassa “atītā saṅkhārā niruddhā, paccuppannā nirujjhanti, anāgate nibbattanakasaṅkhārāpi evameva nirujjhissantī” ti passato etasmiṃ ṭhāne bhayatupaṭṭhānañāṇaṃ nāma uppajjati. Aparāpi upamā – ekā kira pūtipajā itthī dasa dārake vijāyi. Tesu nava matā, eko hatthagato marati, aparo kucchiyaṃ. Sā nava dārake mate dasamañca mīyamānaṃ disvā kucchigate ālayaṃ vissajji “ayampi etesaññeva sadiso bhavissatī” ti. Tattha tassā itthiyā navannaṃ dārakānaṃ maraṇānussaraṇaṃ viya yogino atītasaṅkhārānaṃ nirodhadassanaṃ, hatthagatassa mīyamānabhāvadassanaṃ viya yogino paccuppannānaṃ nirodhadassanaṃ, kucchigate ālayavissajjanaṃ viya anāgatānaṃ nirodhadassanaṃ. Tassevaṃ passato etasmiṃ khaṇe uppajjati bhayatupaṭṭhānañāṇaṃ. 751. Bhayatupaṭṭhānañāṇaṃ pana bhāyati na bhāyatīti? Na bhāyati. Tañhi atītā saṅkhārā niruddhā, paccuppannā nirujjhanti, anāgatā nirujjhissantīti tīraṇamattameva hoti. Tasmā yathā nāma cakkhumā puriso nagaradvāre tisso aṅgārakāsuyo olokayamāno sayaṃ na bhāyati, kevalaṃ hissa “ye ye ettha nipatissanti, sabbe anappakaṃ dukkhamanubhavissantī” ti tīraṇamattameva hoti.**  
> 750. As he thus often develops, cultivates, and makes much of the contemplation of dissolution, which has as its object the cessation consisting in the exhaustion, fall, and breakup of all formations, then formations in all kinds of becoming, generation, destiny, station, or abode of beings appear to him as a great terror—just as lions, tigers, leopards, bears, hyenas, spirits, ogres, fierce bulls, savage dogs, rut-maddened wild elephants, hideous venomous serpents, thunderbolts, charnel grounds, battlefields, and flaming coal pits appear to a timid man who wants to live happily. As he sees that “past formations have ceased, present ones are ceasing, and those to be generated in the future will cease in just the same way,” what is called knowledge of appearance as terror (bhayatupaṭṭhānañāṇa) arises in him at that stage. Another simile: it is said that a certain woman with fetid offspring (pūtipajā) gave birth to ten children. Among them, nine died, one was dying in her hands, and another was in her womb. Seeing the nine children dead and the tenth one dying, she gave up hope for the one in her womb, thinking, “This one too will be just like them.” In this simile, the practitioner’s seeing the cessation of past formations is like the woman’s recollection of the death of the nine children; his seeing the cessation of present ones is like her seeing the dying state of the one in her hands; and his seeing the cessation of future ones is like her giving up hope for the one in her womb. When he sees in this way, knowledge of appearance as terror arises in him at that moment. 751. But does the knowledge of appearance as terror fear or does it not? It does not fear. For it is only the mere judgment that past formations have ceased, present ones are ceasing, and future ones will cease. Therefore, just as a man with vision looking at three charcoal pits at a town gate does not himself fear, for he only forms the mere judgment that “whoever falls in here will all experience much suffering”;
