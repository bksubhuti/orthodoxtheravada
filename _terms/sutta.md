### Grammatical Analysis
**Sutta**: [nt.] discourse; sermon; thread; aphorism. The Aṭṭhakathā explains the word by six reasons: because it indicates meanings (*sūcanato*), because it is well-spoken (*suvuttato*), because it flows (*savanato*), because it distils (*sūdanato*), because it protects (*suttāṇā*), and because it is like a thread or guideline (*suttasabhāgato*) (As 158-159; Sv-i 127-129).

### Orthodox Definition
A *Sutta* is a discourse of the *Suttanta Piṭaka*, the second basket of the canon, and the first of the nine divisions (*navaṅga*) of the Teaching: *sutta, geyya, veyyākaraṇa, gāthā, udāna, itivuttaka, jātaka, abbhutadhamma, vedalla* (AN 4.102). Most suttas are spoken by the Buddha, but the canon also contains discourses spoken by great disciples, such as the *Sammādiṭṭhi Sutta* (MN 9), which Venerable Sāriputta addressed to the monks (M-i 238:2). Collectively they form the *Sutta Piṭaka*, whose collections are the five Nikāyas (Sv-i 173:2).

The Aṭṭhakathā classifies the Suttanta Piṭaka as *vohāra-desanā* (teaching by convention), because the Blessed One, skilled in convention, taught it mostly in conventional terms (As 181:2; Sv-i 149:3). The meanings in a sutta are "well-spoken" because they are spoken in accordance with the inclinations of those to be trained (*veneyyajjhāsayānulomena*; Sv-i 129:2). They make extensive use of conventional terms like "person," "being," "soul," "[deva](/glossary/deva/)," and "king" to anchor moral and meditative lessons. 

However, in Orthodox Theravāda, the suttas are inextricably linked to the [Abhidhamma](/glossary/abhidhamma/). The suttas provide an accessible framework, but they can only be deeply understood through the technical lens of the Abhidhamma. The Abhidhamma analyzes these exact same teachings in terms of ultimate realities (*paramattha-dhamma*). 

Additionally, suttas are of two kinds, *nītattha* (meaning already drawn out) and *neyyattha* (meaning that has to be drawn out), and to take one for the other is to misrepresent the Tathāgata (AN 2.25, A-ii 64:3). The Aṭṭhakathā gives "Monks, there is one person…" as an example of a *neyyattha* sutta, since in the ultimate sense there is no person (Mp-ii 124:3-4). Because of this, the Commentaries (*[Aṭṭhakathā](/glossary/atthakatha/)*) are essential. They give the background story for each discourse and tell us who was listening. The Commentaries also preserve the exact orthodox definitions of words. Without this guide, a reader might easily misunderstand a sutta or lose its deeper meaning.  Most translators use the Commentaries to accurately translate the meaning of the suttas.

### Textual References
* **Canonical**: The five great collections: *Dīgha Nikāya*, *Majjhima Nikāya*, *Saṃyutta Nikāya*, *Aṅguttara Nikāya*, and *Khuddaka Nikāya* (Sv-i 173:2); and the nine divisions beginning with *sutta* (AN 4.102, A-iv 985:2).
* **Commentary**: The individual *Aṭṭhakathās* authored by Ācariya Buddhaghosa (e.g., *[Papañcasūdanī](/glossary/papancasudani/)* for the *Majjhima Nikāya* and *[Sumaṅgalavilāsinī](/glossary/sumangalavilasini/)* for the *Dīgha Nikāya*); the definition of the word *sutta* is at As 158-159 and Sv-i 127-129.
