### Grammatical Analysis
**Āhāre paṭikūlasaññā**: [f.] the perception of repulsiveness in nutriment/food. Formed by *āhāre* (in food/nutriment) + *paṭikūla* (repulsive, disagreeable, disgusting) + *[saññā](/glossary/sanna/)* (perception).

### Orthodox Definition
*Āhāre paṭikūlasaññā* is a highly specialized meditation subject (*kammaṭṭhāna*) designed exclusively to shatter the hindrance of sensual desire (*[kāmacchanda](/glossary/kamachanda/)*), specifically the primal attachment to the taste of food (*rasa-taṇhā*).

The practitioner deliberately alters their perception of a meal by analyzing it through ten repulsive aspects:
1. Going (*gamana*: the dirty path walked to collect alms).
2. Seeking (*pariyesana*: the exhaustion of begging).
3. Using (*paribhoga*: the foul mixing of chewed food with saliva, resembling dog vomit in a dog's trough).
4. Abode (*āsaya*: the four secretional abodes: bile, phlegm, pus, blood).
5. Repository (*nidhāna*: the stomach where it is stored).
6. Undigested (*aparipakka*).
7. Digested (*paripakka*).
8. Fruit (*phala*).
9. Outflow (*nissanda*).
10. Smearing (*sammakkhana*).

By meditating on this, the monk eats purely for the maintenance of the body, completely devoid of lust or pride. It produces Access Concentration (*upacāra-samādhi*).

### Quote

Āhāre paṭikūlasaññāparicitena, bhikkhave, bhikkhuno cetasā bahulaṃ viharato rasataṇhāya cittaṃ patilīyati … pe … upekkhā vā pāṭikulyatā vā saṇṭhāti. ‘ Āhāre paṭikūlasaññā, bhikkhave, bhāvitā bahulīkatā mahapphalā hoti mahānisaṃsā amatogadhā amatapariyosānā ’ ti, iti yaṃ taṃ vuttaṃ idametaṃ paṭicca vuttaṃ.**  
> “‘The perception of the foulness in food, monks, when developed and pursued, is of great fruit, of great benefit. It gains a footing in the deathless, has the deathless as its final end’: Thus indeed was it stated. For what reason was this stated? Monks, when a bhikkhu’s mind often remains steeped in the perception of the foulness in food, his mind shrinks away from craving for flavors … and either equanimity or revulsion becomes established. ‘The perception of the foulness in food, monks, when developed and pursued, is of great fruit, of great benefit. It gains a footing in the deathless, has the deathless as its final end’: Thus was it stated, and for this reason was it stated.

### Textual References
* **Canonical**: *Aṅguttara Nikāya* (Sattaka Nipāta, AN 7.49, Dutiyasaññā Sutta) – Lists the perception of the foulness in food (*āhāre paṭikūlasaññā*) among the seven perceptions that gain a footing in the deathless, and explains it as turning the mind away from craving for flavors (*rasataṇhā*).
* **Commentary**: *[Visuddhimagga](/glossary/visuddhimagga/)* (Chapter XI) – The brilliantly graphic, step-by-step manual on how to strip the glamour away from fine dining to reveal its biological reality.
